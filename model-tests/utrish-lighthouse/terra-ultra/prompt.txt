THE PLACE
Utrish Island is a small, steep, rocky island separated from the Bolshoy Utrish cape by a strait only about 20 m wide. A narrow metal bridge crosses the strait, and a footpath runs along a low causeway from the bridge to the lighthouses. The island's shores are mostly sheer rock, so nobody can walk around it at the water's edge. Geologically the cape and island are the tongue of a huge ancient landslide ("Utrish" is usually translated as "landslide" or "collapsed mountain"), so the rock should read as broken, tilted, fractured strata of light grey-ochre limestone and marl, with fallen blocks at the waterline.

On the island stand two lighthouses:
- The 1975 lighthouse-monument, about 17 m tall, designed by Anapa architect Boris Kamaev. It is a memorial to the Black Sea sailors of the Second World War. A sculpted relief band runs around the tower. Nearby is a granite slab with memorial names. Show it as a slender pale tower with the relief band, a gallery and a lantern on top.
- The low 1911 lighthouse a short distance from the shoreline, converted into the small Chapel of St. Nicholas, patron saint of sailors, with a small dome. Show it in its pre-2025 state, before the chapel's relocation began.
Keep the scene respectful: this is a memorial site.

Across the strait, the Bolshoy Utrish cape has a pebble beach, a small pier for pleasure boats, and a few moored motorboats and a small excursion boat. Behind them, the ridges of the Abrau peninsula rise steeply. They are covered in the reserve's relict, Mediterranean-looking open woodland: dark-green juniper, pistachio and Pitsunda pine, with pale rock showing through and dry summer grass. Hint at the edge of the open-air dolphinarium lagoon with its floating net pens, at a modest scale. A few small buildings of the village may appear at the edge, but keep the focus on the island, the strait and the sea.

THE SEA
The sea is the main visual attraction, and it should feel like the Black Sea: no tides; very clear, slightly green-tinted water over pebbles and rock; quickly deepening blue offshore. On the open western side, swells roll in from the open sea, steepen against the island's cliffs and fallen blocks, burst into white foam, wash over low rock ledges and drain back. In the narrow strait, the water surges and sloshes between the walls. In the lee of the island and along the pebble beach of the cape, the water is calm, with small ripples and high clarity, revealing rounded pebbles, boulders and patches of seagrass and brown algae. The colour should shift naturally from deep blue to blue-green to transparent shallows. Keep the motion coherent: the sheltered beach is visibly calmer than the exposed side of the island.

Use coastal vegetation sparingly: tufts of dry grass on the island, small wildflowers in crevices, junipers clinging to the cliff edges of the cape.
